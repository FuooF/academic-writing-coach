# Revision rubric

Use the writer's material to answer these questions. A missing answer identifies a revision or an information need; it is not a reason to invent a fact.

| Dimension | Question to resolve | Observable improvement |
|---|---|---|
| Paragraph purpose | What should the reader understand or accept after this paragraph? | A discernible point supported by the remaining sentences. |
| Evidence | Which supplied observation or source supports each claim? | Claims keep their support, scope, uncertainty, and citation markers. |
| Organization | What must the reader know before the next sentence makes sense? | Necessary definitions, comparisons, and context precede their use. |
| Synthesis | Are sources compared on a shared question, population, method, or finding? | Relations between studies replace a disconnected sequence of summaries. |
| Precision | Which terms name stable technical concepts? | Terminology stays consistent; vague pronouns and unspecified comparisons are resolved when the source permits. |
| Commitment | Does the verb express the strength of the evidence? | No accidental change from association to causation, possibility to certainty, or absence of significance to equality. |
| Flow | What relation connects adjacent sentences or paragraphs? | A transition names a real relation without adding an unsupported conclusion. |
| Economy and voice | Which words duplicate a point, and which preserve the author's meaning or voice? | Redundancy decreases while meaningful nuance and disciplinary terms remain. |

For a learning task, explain a decision in terms the writer can reuse: identify the signal, explain the choice, then ask the writer to apply it in a new passage. Do not equate sophisticated vocabulary, longer sentences, more citations, or a lower similarity score with stronger writing.

For an effect evaluation, keep semantic preservation separate from fluency. A fluent edit that changes a number, evidence level, citation relationship, or causal interpretation fails that preservation check.
